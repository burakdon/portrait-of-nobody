Portrait of Somebody, Maybe? Idk

Burak, 2026 Sixty simulated sitters and one invented face

Sixty people sat for this portrait but we can appreciate none of them.

Reading the privacy and fairness chapters of Moral AI, I kept thinking that AI safeguards and protections lose whenever they stand in the way of technical and commercial progress. So the idea that interests me most is the one where the two stop competing: synthetic data.

Each sitter here is drawn faintly and laid over all the others, until only what they share is left. The bright face on top is drawn from that shared shape. It looks like a person and it belongs to no one. You can study it, copy it and pass it around, and nobody has been exposed. Press the button and another nobody takes its place.

That is what I hope truly representative synthetic data can be: all of the patterns, and none of the people.

I met the same idea from the other side while building NeuroCue with my team. We wanted to train an AI speech therapist for people with aphasia, and it needed thousands of practice sessions that no real patient should have to sit through. So we built synthetic patients, fitted to how real patients speak, and let it practise on people who do not exist.

One sitter does not blend in. Look for the face that leans away from the rest and stays visible after the others have dissolved. A portrait made from everyone still has to decide what to do with the person who is unlike everyone else.

The portrait is also more hopeful than the real thing. Synthetic data is drawn from a model, and a model keeps traces of what it learned from. Researchers have shown that the distribution can be worked backwards. With enough samples, an attacker can tell whether a particular person was in the training data, and image generators have been caught reproducing near-copies of pictures they were trained on. The more faithfully the synthetic data follows the real people, the more of them can be recovered from it.

So the bright face is a promise more than a guarantee. Defences exist, such as deliberately adding noise while the model learns, and each one costs some of the accuracy that made the data worth having. I still think this is the most promising place for privacy and progress to meet. It only works if we keep checking whether the nobody in the picture can be traced back to a somebody.

A creative reflection on Moral AI: And How We Get There by Jana Schaich Borg, Walter Sinnott-Armstrong and Vincent Conitzer. The sitters are generated by code. No real faces or personal data were used.
